Starting a blog might seem like a really random move for a junior developer like me. I am just fresh out of college, navigating the chaotic transition from structured syllabi to the open-ended world of professional software development. To be honest, it feels random to me, too. If you had told me three years ago that I would voluntarily sit down and write over a thousand words about random things I would have laughed at you.

But here we are.

The number one reason why I'm doing this, and the reason I’m actually hitting "publish" instead of letting this sit in a draft folder forever is simply to clear my mind. The last few years have been a chaotic mess of learning, coding, apply to jobs, interviewing, and trying to figure out where I fit in this world. That's a lot of noise. Writing helps with my mental state in a way most things doesn't. When I code, I am making things happen on a computer, when I write, I am taking noise in my head and putting it in the world. Putting my thoughts out there, even if no one reads them, feels therapeutic.

## Writing in School

I’ve never really been a big fan of writing. Growing up, and especially when I was in school, I viewed writing like a chore. It was always transactional. I wrote because there was an assignment, a rubric, and a deadline. I wrote to prove I had read a book I didn’t care about, or to argue a point I didn’t actually believe in, just to pass the class. It was meaningless, something that I had to do rather than enjoyed.

But as I settled into life after graduating, that feeling started to shift. The noise of the "real world" is different from the endless yapping of the classroom. There are no professors giving you assignments anymore. There are no deadlines for expressing yourself.

Now, I have this strange urge to just write. I want to create something that consists purely of my own thoughts, putting my mind forward without a rubric to judge me. Perhaps it’s because now I’m not forced to do it. Doing it out of my own volition changes the entire dynamic of the activity. It’s no longer just homework, it’s a something I want to do. It’s no longer about satisfying a teacher, it’s about satisfying my brain, even if I’m just shouting into the digital void. I’m realizing that writing isn’t just for English majors or journalists, it’s a tool for thinking, and right now, I have a lot of thinking to do.

## The Developer's Approach: Building vs. Using

Of course, I couldn't just sign up for a WordPress or Medium account and call it a day. That would be too easy, and as a developer, I'm apparently allergic to the easy route.

I decided to use my experience in developing web applications to build my own blog page from the ground up. This decision was partially practical, partially just for the fun of it, and honestly, a little bit dumb too. I wanted to make it exactly the way I like it, free from being restricted to the rules of standard blogging platforms.

When you use a platform like Medium or Substack, you are renting space in someone else’s house. You have to live with their design choices, their paywalls, and their algorithms. You don’t own anything, and you certainly don’t control the <div> tags. For a writer, that’s fine. For a web developer, it’s a peeve.

There is a distinct joy in coding for yourself. In a professional setting, code is a means to a business end. It has to be scalable, maintainable by a team, and shipped yesterday. Here, on this blog, the code is a playground. If I want to rewrite the entire backend because of a fancy new framework I'm interested in, I can. If I want to break the site for a day while I tinker with a new library, I don't have to convince my boss. This blog is as much a technical portfolio as it is a personal diary. It stands as proof that I can build a full-stack application, handle deployment, manage domains, and ship a product—even if the "product" is just a container for my ramblings.

## What’s Next?

So, what is next for this blog?

In the foreseeable future, I plan to simply keep writing and keep posting whenever something comes up. There won't be a strict schedule as to how much I post and when I post. I know the common advice for new bloggers is "consistency is key." They tell you to post every Tuesday at 10 AM, to have a content calendar, to niche down.

I am ignoring all of that.

I am intentionally choosing inconsistency. If I force myself to write on a schedule, I know exactly what will happen: I will burn out and stop writing by next month. And the moment it feels like an obligation, the urge to write will vanish, replaced by the same feeling I felt in high school English class.

I am going to post essentially just whenever I feel like writing something. Some weeks that might mean three posts about a random new obsession, or a rant about a video game I’m playing. Other times, there might be radio silence for a month while I focus on life or work. I want this space to be a reflection of my actual state of mind.

Content-wise, expect a mix. I am a junior developer, so naturally, there will be technical speak. I’ll likely share snippets of things I’m learning, tutorials for problems that took me way too long to solve, and opinions on the tech industry that I’ll probably regret in five years. But I also want to write about life outside of just code. I want to talk about the feeling of being in your 20s, the anxiety of the future, hobbies, media, and the mundane things that make up a life. I don't want to be just a "tech blog." I want to be a person who blogs.

## The Monetization Dream (However Small)

I also hope in the future I might be able to monetize this blog with Adsense or something similar. I’m not delusional, I don’t expect to quit my job and blog full-time from a mansion. I know the CPM rates for personal blogs are negligible, and I'm aware that my traffic will likely remain in the double digits for a long time.

But the goal isn’t wealth, it’s sustainability. I want to see if I can make a little extra money from this, just enough to cover the cost of a coffee now and then. There is something incredibly validating about earning even a single dollar from something you created entirely by yourself. In my day job, I get a paycheck, but that money comes from being a cog of a massive machine. It’s compensation for time, not necessarily for creation.

If somehow it goes well, and I start seeing a trickle of revenue, it might even be a good motivator for me to keep writing. Money is a powerful gamification tool. Watching a graph go up, even slowly, hits the same dopamine receptors as leveling up in a game. It provides a tangible metric of progress. And I mean, who doesn't like a little extra income? It would prove that my thoughts have value, that my little corner of the internet is providing something worth a fraction of a cent to someone, somewhere.

## Conclusion

So, that’s the plan. my own platform, a random posting schedule, and a hope for some pocket change. It’s not the most professional plan, but it’s an honest one.

For now, the site is live. The code is deployed. The first post is written. The cursor is blinking on the blank page of the future, and I’m excited to see what I have to say.

Thanks for reading, and welcome to my stream of consciousness.
